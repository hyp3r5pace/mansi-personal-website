Madder root is a slow teacher. You can rush the indigo vat — twenty minutes
in, twenty minutes out, oxidise, repeat — but madder asks for a full
afternoon at a low simmer, and then another morning of rinsing. Skip a step
and the rose drifts brick.

I spent three weeks in Bagru this spring trialling combinations of madder
and marigold[^1] over hand-spun cotton. The notebook is full of small
disasters: an overdyed swatch the colour of wet earth, a marigold bath that
broke on me because I let it boil[^2]. The good swatches are the boring
ones, which is the lesson I keep relearning.

## What I changed

A few process notes, mostly to remind myself next time:

- **Mordant the night before.** Alum on dry cotton at room temperature, eight
  hours minimum. Anything faster cheats the binding.
- **Two short baths beat one long one.** The colour deepens in layers; a
  single long bath flattens it.
- **Rinse cold, dry slow.** Hot rinse pulls the dye back out. Direct sun
  yellows the madder over a week — better to dry in shade.

> The studio is not a kitchen. You're not cooking. You're convincing.

That line is from Asha-ji, who has been dyeing for forty years and has the
patience of a woman who knows the colour will be there if she waits.

## On scheduling

The real lesson wasn't about colour at all. It was about how long things
actually take when you stop fighting them. A natural-dye capsule needs
three weeks of calendar, not three weeks of effort — fabric has to rest, dye
baths have to cool, swatches have to dry in the shade.

I've started padding studio timelines accordingly. Two weeks of "doing" and
one week of "waiting" is now the default for anything involving dye.

[^1]: Local marigold from the temple offerings at Galta-ji, dried and stored
  in muslin. The colour is closer to saffron than to the imported
  Mexican variety.
[^2]: Marigold breaks above 75°C. The bath turns from a clear yellow to a
  cloudy olive and nothing you do brings it back.
